Purification & Quantitative/Qualitative Analysis

Class 11 • Practical Organic Chemistry • Core JEE Advanced Focus

1. Core Concepts Summary

Purification techniques isolate pure organic substrates based on discrepancies in phase partition, volatility, solubility, and vapor pressure. Qualitative analysis breaks organic matrices down via active elemental transformations (e.g., Lassaigne’s reductive fusion into ionic species) to identify N, S, halogens, and phosphorus via precipitation and coordination complexes. Quantitative analysis employs stoichiometrically complete transformations (Liebig combustion, Kjeldahl digestion, Dumas combustion, and Carius mineralizations) to determine empirical/molecular formulations.

2. Key Processes & Stepwise Mechanisms

A. Lassaigne’s Sodium Fusion Extract Preparation

Insoluble covalent heteroatoms (N, S, X) are converted to water-soluble ionic sodium salts by reductive thermal cleavage with metallic sodium.

  1. Thermal Cleavage & Fusion: Na + C + N → NaCN; 2Na + S → Na2S; Na + X → NaX (where X = Cl, Br, I).
  2. Combined N & S Extraction: If both N and S are present, Na + C + N + S → NaSCN (sodium thiocyanate). If excess Na is used: NaSCN + 2Na → NaCN + Na2S.
  3. Acidification/Boiling step: Before testing halogens, extract must be boiled with concentrated HNO3 to decompose CN− and S2− into HCN↑ and H2S↑, preventing precipitation of interfering AgCN and Ag2S.

Rate/Efficiency Determiner: Complete reduction during fusion. Unreacted metallic Na must be quenched in ethanol/water before leaching.

C. Kjeldahl Digestion & Distillation

Step 1: Digestion (RDS): Organic Nitrogen → (NH4)2SO4 using conc. H2SO4.

Catalysts: CuSO4 (catalyst), K2SO4 (elevates boiling point of H2SO4).

Step 2: Neutralization & Distillation: (NH4)2SO4 + 2NaOH → Na2SO4 + 2H2O + 2NH3↑.

Step 3: Absorption & Back/Direct Titration: NH3 is absorbed into known excess standard H2SO4 (or boric acid) and back-titrated against standard NaOH.

4. Comparative Physical Orders & Underlying Principles

Chromatographic Adsorption Affinity (Silica gel / Alumina Stationary Phase)

Order: Hydrocarbons (alkanes) < Alkenes < Dienes < Aromatic Hydrocarbons < Halocarbons < Ethers < Esters < Ketones < Aldehydes < Amines < Alcohols < Phenols < Carboxylic Acids.

Reason: Adsorption strength on polar stationary surfaces increases with compound dipole moment, hydrogen-bonding capability, and Lewis-base coordination strength. Less polar eluents wash down nonpolar fractions faster (higher Rf value).

Steam Volatility (Intramolecular vs. Intermolecular H-Bonding)

Order of Steam Distillation Rate: o-Nitrophenol > p-Nitrophenol (or m-nitrophenol).

Reason: Chelation (intramolecular H-bonding) in o-nitrophenol suppresses association with surrounding solvent and prevents self-oligomerization, increasing its partial vapor pressure (Po). p-Nitrophenol forms extensive intermolecular hydrogen-bond networks, raising its boiling point and diminishing volatility in steam.

Silver Halide Solubility in Aqueous Ammonia (NH4OH)

Order of Dissolution: AgCl (completely soluble in dilute NH4OH) > AgBr (sparingly soluble in concentrated NH4OH) > AgI (completely insoluble in NH4OH).

Reason: Ksp drops drastically: Ksp(AgCl) ≈ 1.8 × 10−10 > Ksp(AgBr) ≈ 5.4 × 10−13 > Ksp(AgI) ≈ 8.5 × 10−17. The formation constant (β2) for [Ag(NH3)2]+ is only sufficient to overcome the lattice free energy of AgCl and, to an extent, AgBr with excess ammonia.

Rf (Retardation Factor) in Normal-Phase Thin-Layer Chromatography

Order: Polar Analytes < Moderately Polar Analytes < Nonpolar Analytes (Rf → 1).

Reason: Rf = (distance traveled by substance) / (distance traveled by solvent front). Because the stationary phase is polar (silica gel with Si−OH groups), polar substrates interact more strongly via dipole-dipole and H-bonding interactions, resulting in minimal migration compared to non-polar analytes.

5. Common Traps & Exceptions (JEE Advanced Pitfalls)

TRAP 1: The Kjeldahl Structural Blacklist
Compounds containing Nitro (−NO2), Diazo / Azo (−N=N−), or Ring-bound Nitrogen (Pyridine, Quinoline, Pyrrole, Piperidine) do NOT quantitatively yield (NH4)2SO4 on digestion with conc. H2SO4. Nitrogen in these frameworks is either oxidized to nitrogen oxides/N2 gas or cannot be cleaved into ammonia under regular Kjeldahl conditions.
TRAP 2: Omission of Boiling Lassaigne Extract with Conc. HNO3
If a sample contains both Nitrogen and Halogens, adding AgNO3 directly to the alkaline extract without boiling with conc. HNO3 gives a curdy white precipitate of AgCN (or black Ag2S if sulfur is present). This mimics AgCl and gives a false positive for chlorine. Nitric acid decomposes cyanide and sulfide ions:
NaCN + HNO3 → NaNO3 + HCN↑ and Na2S + 2HNO3 → 2NaNO3 + H2S↑.
TRAP 3: False Positives in Beilstein's Wire Test
Beilstein test is extremely sensitive to halogens, but compounds like Urea, Thiourea, Pyridine derivatives, and Bipyridyls also yield volatile copper cyanide complexes that impart a transient brilliant green/blue flame without containing any halogen atoms.
TRAP 4: Condition for Steam Distillation Suitability
Steam distillation requires an organic substance to be completely immiscible with water, have an appreciable vapor pressure (typically ≥ 10–15 mm Hg at 373 K), and be thermally stable at ~100 °C. Total pressure is Ptotal = Pwater + Porganic = 1 atm; hence the mixture distills at a temperature lower than the normal boiling points of either water or the pure organic liquid.

6. Practice Questions (JEE Advanced Level)

Q1. [Conceptual] Why is dilute H2SO4 preferred over dilute HCl or HNO3 for acidifying the Lassaigne extract when detecting Nitrogen as Prussian Blue?
Answer: HCl and HNO3 interfere directly with complexation and redox steps.
Reasoning: 1. HNO3 is a strong oxidizing agent; it oxidizes Fe2+ to Fe3+ uncontrollably and can oxidize unreacted cyanide (CN−) or generate thiocyanate species if sulfur is present.
2. HCl introduces Cl− ions which can form yellow tetrachloroferrate(III) complexes [FeCl4]−, masking the true Prussian Blue Fe4[Fe(CN)6]3 precipitate.
3. Dilute H2SO4 provides non-interfering SO42− ions, purely serving to dissolve the unwanted Fe(OH)2 and Fe(OH)3 precipitates without interfering side reactions.
Q2. [Conceptual] Under what specific conditions does Lassaigne's test for an organic compound containing both N and S yield a Prussian blue precipitate instead of a blood-red color?
Answer: When fusion is performed with an excess of metallic sodium.
Reasoning: With equimolar or deficient sodium, sodium thiocyanate (NaSCN) forms, giving blood-red [Fe(SCN)3] with Fe3+. However, when metallic sodium is in excess, it reduces NaSCN further:
NaSCN + 2Na → NaCN + Na2S
The generated NaCN then undergoes classic hexacyanoferrate(II) formation followed by Prussian blue precipitation upon reaction with iron ions.
Q3. [Conceptual] Explain why hydrazine (NH2−NH2) and hydroxylamine (NH2OH) fail to give the Lassaigne test for nitrogen despite containing nitrogen atoms.
Answer: Both compounds lack carbon atoms.
Reasoning: The Lassaigne test relies on the simultaneous presence of carbon and nitrogen to synthesize cyanide ions via the reaction:
Na + C + N → NaCN
Without carbon, hydrazine simply decomposes/volatilizes or forms sodamide/ammonia without producing NaCN, giving zero Prussian Blue precipitate.
Q4. [Conceptual] In paper chromatography, what acts as the stationary phase and mobile phase respectively, and what is the primary separation mechanism?
Answer: Stationary phase: Water trapped in the cellulose fibers of the paper pores. Mobile phase: Developing organic solvent. Mechanism: Liquid-liquid partition chromatography.
Reasoning: Although the paper is solid cellulose, the hydroxyl networks tightly retain a stable aqueous monolayer. The migrating analytes continuously partition between this bound water layer and the ascending mobile organic solvent phase according to their partition coefficients.
Q5. [Product Prediction] In a Carius estimation of halogens, an organic compound containing bromine is heated with fuming HNO3 and excess AgNO3. What is the formula of the precipitate, its color, and its behavior upon addition of concentrated NH4OH?
Answer: Precipitate: AgBr; Color: Pale yellow; Behavior: Sparingly soluble in concentrated NH4OH.
Reasoning: AgBr dissolves only partially in conc. NH4OH to establish equilibrium with the diamminesilver(I) complex ion:
AgBr(s) + 2NH3(aq) ⇌ [Ag(NH3)2]+(aq) + Br−(aq).
Q6. [Product Prediction] An organic sulfur-bearing compound is fused with sodium peroxide (Na2O2), extracted with water, acidified with dilute HCl, and treated with barium chloride. Identify the ionic transformations and final precipitate.
Answer: The sulfur oxidizes to sulfate (SO42−), yielding a insoluble white precipitate of barium sulfate (BaSO4).
Equations:
1. Organic [S] + Na2O2 → Na2SO4
2. Na2SO4 + BaCl2 → BaSO4↓ (white, completely insoluble in hot dilute mineral acids) + 2NaCl.
Q7. [Product Prediction] Treatment of Lassaigne extract with fresh sodium nitroprusside yields an intense violet chromophore. Write the complete coordination formula of the reagent and the violet complex formed.
Answer: Reagent: [Fe(CN)5(NO)]2− (Sodium nitroprusside, containing Fe2+ and nitrosyl NO+);
Violet complex: [Fe(CN)5(NOS)]4− (Sodium thionitroprusside).
Equation: Na2S + Na2[Fe(CN)5(NO)] → Na4[Fe(CN)5(NOS)].
Q8. [Multi-step Quantitative] 0.25 g of an organic monocarboxylic acid containing only C, H, and O yielded 0.3667 g of CO2 and 0.1500 g of H2O in a Liebig combustion tube. Determine the empirical formula of the compound.
Answer: Empirical formula = C2H4O.
Stepwise Calculation:
1. Mass of Carbon = (12 / 44) × 0.3667 = 0.1000 g
⇒ %C = (0.1000 / 0.25) × 100 = 40.0%
2. Mass of Hydrogen = (2 / 18) × 0.1500 = 0.0167 g
⇒ %H = (0.0167 / 0.25) × 100 = 6.67%
3. %Oxygen = 100 − (40.0 + 6.67) = 53.33%
4. Molar ratios:
  • C: 40.0 / 12 = 3.33
  • H: 6.67 / 1 = 6.67
  • O: 53.33 / 16 = 3.33
5. Divide by lowest (3.33): C = 1, H = 2, O = 1 ⇒ Empirical formula: CH2O (or molecular formula C2H4O2 for acetic acid).
Q9. [Multi-step Quantitative] In a Dumas nitrogen determination, 0.20 g of an organic compound gave 24.6 cm3 of moist nitrogen gas at 300 K and 760 mm Hg total pressure. The aqueous tension of water vapor at 300 K is 15 mm Hg. Calculate the percentage of nitrogen in the compound. (R = 0.0821 L atm K−1 mol−1)
Answer: 13.68% (or ~13.7%).
Stepwise Calculation:
1. Correct for aqueous tension: Pdry N2 = Ptotal − Paq = 760 − 15 = 745 mm Hg = (745 / 760) atm ≈ 0.9803 atm.
2. Convert volume to STP (P0 = 1 atm, T0 = 273 K):
V0 = (P × V × 273) / (760 × T) = (745 × 24.6 × 273) / (760 × 300) = 21.95 mL.
3. Calculate mass of N2:
Mass of N = (28 × 21.95) / 22400 = 0.02744 g.
4. Percentage of Nitrogen:
%N = (0.02744 / 0.20) × 100 = 13.72%.
Q10. [Multi-step Quantitative] 0.40 g of an organic compound was digested according to Kjeldahl's method. The evolved NH3 was absorbed in 50 mL of 0.1 M H2SO4. The unreacted residual acid required 30 mL of 0.1 M NaOH for complete neutralization. Calculate the percentage of nitrogen in the compound.
Answer: 24.5%.
Stepwise Calculation:
1. Total milliequivalents of H2SO4 taken = Normality × Volume = (0.1 × 2) × 50 = 10 meq.
2. Milliequivalents of NaOH consumed in back titration = 0.1 × 30 = 3 meq.
3. Residual acid = 3 meq ⇒ Milliequivalents of H2SO4 neutralized by NH3 = 10 − 3 = 7 meq.
4. Equivalents of NH3 = Equivalents of Nitrogen = 7 × 10−3 mol.
5. Mass of Nitrogen = 7 × 10−3 × 14 = 0.098 g.
6. %N = (0.098 / 0.40) × 100 = 24.5%.
