Hydrocarbons

Class 12 | JEE Advanced

Chapter summary

Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen and are broadly classified as alkanes, alkenes, alkynes and aromatic hydrocarbons. Their reactions are governed by C–C/C–H bond strength, hybridisation, electron density, molecular geometry and reaction intermediates. Alkanes mainly undergo free-radical substitution, while alkenes and alkynes commonly undergo electrophilic addition. Aromatic compounds favour electrophilic aromatic substitution because substitution preserves aromaticity. JEE Advanced questions frequently combine nomenclature, stability of intermediates, regioselectivity, stereochemistry, reaction mechanisms and multi-step conversions.

sp3 C–H < sp2 C–H < sp C–H

Alkene structure, stability and addition

FeatureExplanation

For simple acyclic alkenes, a useful stability trend is:

tetrasubstituted > trisubstituted > disubstituted > monosubstituted
CHECK: Alkene stability comparisons can be affected by conjugation, ring strain and steric effects. The substitution trend is not a universal rule for every possible pair of alkenes.

Aromaticity and benzene

Aromaticity is a special stabilisation associated with a cyclic, planar, continuously conjugated system containing an appropriate number of π electrons. For the common monocyclic case, Hückel's rule is:

Number of π electrons = 4n + 2, where n = 0, 1, 2, ...
RequirementMeaning
CyclicThe relevant p orbitals form a closed ring.
PlanarAdjacent p orbitals can overlap effectively.
ConjugatedContinuous overlap of p orbitals around the ring.
4n + 2 π electronsGives the aromatic electron count for the standard Hückel treatment.
CHECK: Hückel's 4n + 2 rule is applied to cyclic, planar, fully conjugated systems. Applying it to an arbitrary molecule without first checking these conditions can give the wrong conclusion.

The key mechanistic intermediate is the σ-complex (arenium ion). Loss of H+ restores the aromatic π system.

Aromatic substitution is favoured over ordinary addition because the substitution pathway restores aromaticity after the electrophilic attack.

Common traps and exceptions

Trap 1: Markovnikov's rule predicts orientation, not necessarily the entire mechanism. Check whether the reaction is ionic or radical before applying it.
Trap 2: The peroxide effect is classically applicable to HBr, not as a blanket rule for HCl and HI.
Trap 3: Halogens deactivate benzene toward electrophilic substitution but direct incoming electrophiles to ortho/para positions.
Trap 4: More substituted alkenes are generally more stable, but conjugation can outweigh simple substitution when comparing different structures.
Trap 5: A terminal alkyne is appreciably more acidic than an alkene or alkane because its conjugate base places charge on an sp-hybridised carbon.
Trap 6: In carbocation mechanisms, do not stop after forming the first carbocation. Check for possible 1,2-hydride or 1,2-alkyl rearrangement before predicting the product.

Practice questions

1. Which statements are correct? (Multiple correct) (A) Alkene carbons are approximately sp2-hybridised. (B) Terminal alkynes are more acidic than alkenes. (C) Rotation around a C=C bond is freely possible at room temperature. (D) A C≡C bond contains two π bonds.

Answer: A, B and D.

Solution: Alkene carbons are sp2; terminal alkynes have sp-hybridised acidic C–H bonds; a triple bond contains one σ and two π bonds. Rotation around C=C is restricted because it would disrupt the π bond.

2. Arrange the following C–H bonds in increasing acidity: ethane, ethene and ethyne.

Answer: Ethane < ethene < ethyne.

Solution: Their conjugate bases place the negative charge on sp3, sp2 and sp-hybridised carbon respectively. Greater s-character stabilises the negative charge, so acidity increases in that order.

3. Which is the most stable among the following simple alkyl radicals: CH3•, CH3CH2•, (CH3)2CH•, (CH3)3C•?

Answer: (CH3)3C•.

Solution: For simple alkyl radicals, greater alkyl substitution provides greater hyperconjugative and electron-releasing stabilisation. The usual order is 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl.

4. Benzene undergoes nitration rather than simple addition under ordinary nitration conditions. Explain the mechanistic reason.

Answer: Electrophilic substitution restores aromaticity after the electrophile attacks the ring.

Solution: Electrophilic attack temporarily forms a non-aromatic σ-complex. Loss of H+ restores the aromatic π system. A permanent addition product would sacrifice aromatic stabilisation.

5. Arrange the following alkenes in increasing stability, ignoring unusual ring-strain effects: CH2=CH2, CH3CH=CH2, (CH3)2C=CH2, (CH3)2C=C(CH3)2.

Answer: CH2=CH2 < CH3CH=CH2 < (CH3)2C=CH2 < (CH3)2C=C(CH3)2.

Solution: The degree of alkyl substitution increases from unsubstituted to monosubstituted to disubstituted to tetrasubstituted, increasing hyperconjugative stabilisation.

6. Propene reacts with HBr in the absence of peroxides. Which carbon receives Br in the major product, and why?

Answer: Br attaches to the terminal carbon, giving 2-bromopropane as the major product.

Solution: Protonation occurs so that the more stable secondary carbocation forms at the middle carbon. Bromide then attacks that carbocation. This is the usual Markovnikov orientation.

7. Propene reacts with HBr in the presence of peroxides. Predict the major constitutional product and identify the mechanism responsible.

Answer: 1-Bromopropane is the major product; the reaction follows a radical chain mechanism.

Solution: In the peroxide pathway, Br• adds to the double bond so that the more stable secondary carbon radical is generated. Hydrogen abstraction from HBr then gives the anti-Markovnikov product.

8. An alkene undergoes acid-catalysed hydration and initially forms a secondary carbocation. A 1,2-hydride shift can convert it into a tertiary carbocation. Predict the major alcohol framework and explain the key step.

Answer: The rearranged tertiary-carbocation framework generally leads to the major alcohol after water attack and deprotonation.

Solution: After protonation, the secondary carbocation undergoes a favourable 1,2-hydride shift if that produces a more stable tertiary carbocation. Water then attacks the rearranged carbocation, followed by deprotonation.

9. Benzene is treated with Br2/FeBr3, followed by nitration. Predict the possible major positional relationship between Br and NO2 in the final product and explain the directing effects.

Answer: Bromine is ortho/para directing, so nitration gives predominantly ortho and para products relative to Br, with the para product often favoured sterically in this type of comparison.

Solution: Although Br deactivates the ring by its −I effect, its lone pair can donate by resonance, making it an ortho/para director. Nitration therefore occurs preferentially at those positions.

10. A compound contains an alkyl group attached to benzene. On vigorous oxidation it gives benzoic acid. Which structural feature must the original alkyl group possess? Also give one example of a suitable starting hydrocarbon.

Answer: The carbon directly attached to the benzene ring must have at least one benzylic hydrogen. Toluene, C6H5CH3, is a suitable example.

Solution: Strong oxidation of an alkylbenzene can convert the entire side chain beyond the benzylic carbon into a carboxyl group, provided the benzylic carbon has at least one H. Thus toluene gives benzoic acid.
