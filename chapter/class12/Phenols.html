Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

Class 12 | JEE Advanced

Core ideas

Alcohols contain the -OH group attached to an sp3-hybridised carbon, phenols contain -OH directly attached to an aromatic ring, and ethers contain an R-O-R' linkage. The chapter is governed by the behaviour of the C-O and O-H bonds, the acidity of the O-H proton, substitution/elimination at carbon, and the effect of resonance in phenols and aryl ethers.

For JEE Advanced, the major connections are alcohol classification and reactivity, acid-base behaviour, dehydration and substitution, phenoxide resonance, electrophilic substitution of phenol, and ether cleavage/synthesis.

Comparisons and orders

Acidity of simple alcohols:
H2O > ROH in general, because an alkyl group has a +I effect and destabilises the alkoxide ion. Among alcohols, increased alkyl substitution generally decreases acidity: CH3OH > 1° > 2° > 3° alcohol, with solvent effects considered for precise comparisons.
Phenol vs alcohol acidity:
Phenol is much more acidic than a typical aliphatic alcohol because phenoxide is resonance-stabilised over the aromatic ring. Electron-withdrawing substituents such as -NO2 increase phenol acidity, especially at ortho/para positions; electron-donating groups such as -CH3 decrease it.
Representative phenol acidity order:
p-NO2-phenol and o-NO2-phenol are more acidic than phenol, while p-CH3-phenol is less acidic. For ortho-substituted compounds, steric, inductive, resonance and intramolecular H-bonding effects can compete, so do not use a single simplistic rule for every substituted phenol.
Alcohol substitution reactivity with HX:
For a mechanism dominated by carbocation formation: 3° > 2° > 1° alcohol. For SN2 displacement, steric hindrance reverses the substrate preference: primary substrates react more readily than secondary, while tertiary substrates do not normally undergo SN2.
Dehydration tendency:
3° > 2° > 1° alcohol for ease of acid-catalysed dehydration when a carbocation pathway is available. Carbocation stability and alkene stability both influence the observed product distribution.
Phenol electrophilic substitution:
-OH strongly activates the ring and directs substitution mainly to ortho and para positions. Phenoxide is even more activating than neutral phenol, which is why strongly basic conditions can make phenoxide reactions exceptionally rapid.
Ether cleavage of unsymmetrical ethers:
With excess HI/HBr, cleavage generally occurs at the less hindered alkyl group by SN2. However, if one side is tertiary and can form a stable carbocation, cleavage can occur through SN1 at that side.
Williamson synthesis selectivity:
Use a primary alkyl halide whenever possible. With tertiary halides, the strong alkoxide base commonly causes E2 elimination instead of the desired SN2 reaction.

Common traps and exceptions

Trap 1: Phenol does not behave like an ordinary aliphatic alcohol in every reaction. Its C-O bond has partial double-bond character from resonance and aryl carbon is sp2, so cleavage of Ar-OH is not a normal SN2 process.
Trap 2: Na metal reacts with both alcohols and phenols, but NaOH distinguishes them more effectively: phenol reacts with NaOH because phenol is sufficiently acidic, whereas most ordinary alcohols do not.
Trap 3: Phenol + bromine water gives 2,4,6-tribromophenol readily. Do not automatically apply the usual benzene bromination requirement of a Lewis acid.
Trap 4: Williamson ether synthesis is an SN2 reaction. Tertiary alkyl halides are poor substrates because elimination competes strongly.
Trap 5: Cleavage of anisole with HI gives phenol + CH3I, not iodobenzene + methanol. The aryl C(sp2)-O bond does not undergo normal SN2 displacement.
Trap 6: In acid-catalysed dehydration, carbocation rearrangement can occur for substrates that form rearrangeable carbocations. The initially expected alkene is not always the final major product.
Trap 7: Primary alcohols generally do not undergo Lucas reaction as rapidly as tertiary alcohols because a primary carbocation is highly unstable and SN2 substitution is less favourable under the Lucas conditions.
Trap 8: Kolbe-Schmitt reaction gives mainly ortho-carboxylation under the classical conditions. Do not confuse it with ordinary Kolbe electrolysis or with the Reimer-Tiemann formylation of phenol.
Trap 9: Reimer-Tiemann introduces -CHO, whereas Kolbe-Schmitt introduces -COOH after acidification. Both are important ortho-functionalisation reactions of phenol.
Trap 10: Alcohol acidity should not be judged solely by gas evolution with sodium metal. Both alcohols and phenols can release H2; the difference lies in the equilibrium acidity and reaction with bases such as NaOH.

Practice questions

1. Conceptual: Why is phenol more acidic than ethanol?Phenol loses H+ to form phenoxide, whose negative charge is delocalised into the aromatic ring through resonance. Ethoxide has no comparable resonance stabilisation, so its conjugate base is less stable.
2. Conceptual: Why does Williamson ether synthesis favour primary alkyl halides?The key step is SN2 attack by an alkoxide/phenoxide. Primary carbon has low steric hindrance, allowing backside attack. Secondary substrates can compete with E2, while tertiary substrates generally undergo elimination rather than SN2.
3. Conceptual: Why does anisole cleavage with HI occur at the methyl group rather than at the aryl carbon?SN2 attack at an sp2 aryl carbon is not feasible. I- therefore attacks the methyl group after protonation of the ether oxygen, giving CH3I and phenol.
4. Conceptual: Why is phenol strongly activating toward electrophilic aromatic substitution even though the oxygen is electronegative?The oxygen lone pair can donate electron density into the aromatic ring by resonance. This stabilises the σ-complex formed during ortho/para attack, overcoming the inductive electron-withdrawing effect of oxygen and making the net substituent strongly activating.
5. Product prediction: Phenol is treated with CHCl3/NaOH followed by acidification. What is the major product?o-Hydroxybenzaldehyde (salicylaldehyde) is the major product. This is the Reimer-Tiemann reaction, in which dichlorocarbene generated from CHCl3/base leads predominantly to ortho formylation.
6. Product prediction: Sodium phenoxide is heated with CO2 under pressure and then acidified. Predict the product.Salicylic acid (o-hydroxybenzoic acid) is the major product. This is the Kolbe-Schmitt reaction: phenoxide undergoes ortho carboxylation followed by acidification.
7. Product prediction: Predict the major product of sodium ethoxide with 2-bromo-2-methylpropane.2-Methylpropene is the major product. Sodium ethoxide is a strong base, and the tertiary halide cannot undergo SN2 efficiently; E2 elimination predominates.
8. Multi-step: Convert propan-1-ol into ethoxyethane using an SN2-based route. Give a suitable sequence.Convert propan-1-ol to a suitable primary alkyl halide, such as 1-bromopropane, and react it with sodium ethoxide. However, this gives ethyl propyl ether, not ethoxyethane. Therefore the correct Williamson route to ethoxyethane is CH3CH2Br + NaOCH2CH3 → CH3CH2OCH2CH3. CHECK: The requested starting material and target require an additional carbon-skeleton adjustment.
9. Comparison: Arrange the following in increasing acidity: ethanol, phenol, p-nitrophenol, p-cresol.p-Cresol < ethanol < phenol < p-nitrophenol is not a universally safe order without considering the exact reference pKa values and solvent. The secure qualitative comparisons are: p-nitrophenol is more acidic than phenol because -NO2 stabilises phenoxide; p-cresol is less acidic than phenol because -CH3 is electron-releasing. CHECK: Verify the intended solvent/standard if a strict complete ordering involving ethanol is required.
10. Multi-step: An alcohol A gives rapid turbidity with Lucas reagent, undergoes dehydration readily, and oxidation gives a ketone. Identify the class of A and explain.A is a secondary alcohol. Oxidation of a secondary alcohol gives a ketone, while secondary alcohols undergo acid-catalysed dehydration more readily than primary alcohols. Rapid Lucas turbidity is characteristic of alcohols that undergo substitution readily, with tertiary alcohols generally reacting fastest and secondary alcohols next.
