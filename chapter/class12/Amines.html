Class 12

Amines & Diazonium Salts

Nitrogenous bases, nucleophilic substitutions, elimination cascades, molecular rearrangements, and diazonium synthetic transformations for JEE Advanced.

1. Chapter Overview & Core Principles

Amines represent trivalent nitrogen centers bearing a non-bonding lone pair, establishing them simultaneously as Lewis bases and nucleophiles. The tetrahedral geometry is conformationally dynamic due to pyramidal inversion (nitrogen inversion through a planar transition state, activation energy ~25 kJ/mol), making chiral unconstrained tertiary amines N(R1)(R2)(R3) non-resolvable at ambient temperature.

Aromatic amines (e.g., aniline) exhibit extensive +M delocalisation of the nitrogen lone pair into the aromatic ring, significantly decreasing basicity while drastically activating the ring towards electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS). Arenediazonium salts [ArN2+ X−], stabilized at 0–5°C via resonance, possess the exceptional N2 leaving group, serving as the master gateway connecting aromatic nitro groups to halides, nitriles, phenols, and azo-dyes.

2. Key Reaction Mechanisms

1. Hofmann Bromamide Degradation RDS: Intramolecular Migration

Conversion of 1° amides to 1° amines with loss of one carbon (carbonyl carbon lost as CO32−).

  1. Deprotonation: Hydroxide removes an acidic proton from R−CONH2 generating the resonance-stabilized amidate anion.
  2. Bromination: Attack of amidate on Br2 yields N-bromoamide (R−CONHBr).
  3. Second Deprotonation: Base removes the second proton from N-bromoamide, forming the conjugate base [R−CON−Br]−.
  4. Concerted Rearrangement (RDS): Intramolecular 1,2-migration of the R group with its electron pair from carbonyl carbon to nitrogen, concerted with the displacement of Br−. Forms Alkyl Isocyanate (R−N=C=O) with complete retention of configuration at the migrating chiral center.
  5. Hydrolysis: Attack of H2O/OH− on isocyanate gives an unstable carbamic acid intermediate (R−NHCOOH), which rapidly decarboxylates to yield R−NH2 + CO32−.
✓ Favoured by: Electron-donating groups on aromatic R (accelerates migration to electron-deficient N), strong alkaline media (4 eq KOH per 1 eq Br2).
2. Gabriel Phthalimide Synthesis RDS: SN2 Alkylation

Clean preparation of pure 1° aliphatic amines without contamination from 2° or 3° amines.

  1. Deprotonation: Ethanolic KOH deprotonates phthalimide (pKa ~8.3) to generate potassium phthalimide nucleophile.
  2. SN2 Displacement (RDS): Phthalimide anion attacks an unhindered alkyl halide (R−CH2−X) in an SN2 step, expelling X− to form N-alkylphthalimide.
  3. Cleavage: Alkaline hydrolysis (aq. NaOH) or nucleophilic hydrazinolysis (NH2NH2, Ing-Manske procedure) cleaves both imide C−N bonds, liberating pure 1° alkyl amine and phthalate salt (or phthalhydrazide).
✓ Favoured by: Methyl and unhindered 1° alkyl halides, polar aprotic solvents (DMF, DMSO). Fails completely for aryl and vinyl halides (no SN2) and 3° halides (leads exclusively to E2 alkene).
3. Diazotization of Primary Aromatic Amines RDS: Nitrosonium Generation / Attack

Generation of arenediazonium chloride [Ar−N≡N]+ Cl− at 273–278 K.

  1. Generation of Nitrosonium ion: Protonation of nitrous acid (HONO) in conc. HCl yields [H2O+−NO], which loses water to form the active electrophile, nitrosonium ion (+N=O).
  2. Nucleophilic Attack: Ar−NH2 attacks +N=O to form N-nitrosammonium cation [Ar−NH2−NO]+.
  3. Proton Shifts & Tautomerization: Deprotonation gives N-nitrosamine (Ar−NH−NO), which undergoes rapid prototropic shift to diazohydroxide (Ar−N=N−OH).
  4. Dehydration: Protonation of the −OH group followed by water expulsion yields the resonance-stabilized arenediazonium cation [Ar−N≡N ↔ Ar−N+=N−].
✓ Favoured by: Low temperature (0–5°C) to prevent thermal decomposition via heterolysis to aryl cation, strong mineral acid excess to avoid triazene coupling with unreacted amine.
4. Hofmann Exhaustive Methylation & Elimination RDS: E2 C−H Bond Cleavage

Regioselective formation of the least substituted alkene from quaternary ammonium hydroxides.

  1. Exhaustive Alkylation: Amine is treated with excess CH3I until converted to quaternary ammonium iodide [R−N+(CH3)3] I−.
  2. Silver Oxide Treatment: Wet Ag2O (or AgOH) precipitates AgI(s), yielding quaternary ammonium hydroxide [R−N+(CH3)3] OH− in solution.
  3. E2 Elimination (RDS): Heating drives concerted anti-periplanar E2 elimination. Hydroxide abstracts the most acidic β-hydrogen (from the least sterically hindered β-carbon) because the bulky −N+(CH3)3 leaving group enforces a carbanion-like transition state (Hofmann rule).
✓ Favoured by: Bulky leaving groups (−N+Me3, −S+Me2), high steric hindrance, and presence of acidic β-hydrogens.

4. Master Orders & Comparisons (JEE Advanced)

A. Basicity of Aliphatic Amines in Aqueous Solution

Aqueous basicity is governed by the interplay of three distinct factors: Inductive effect (+I), Solvation/Hydration of cation, and Steric hindrance.

  • Methyl-substituted series: (CH3)2NH > CH3NH2 > (CH3)3N > NH3 (Order: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH3).
    Reason: Small methyl group creates negligible steric crowding, allowing extensive cation hydration, thus 1° edges out 3°.
  • Ethyl-substituted series: (C2H5)2NH > (C2H5)3N > C2H5NH2 > NH3 (Order: 2° > 3° > 1° > NH3).
    Reason: Stronger +I effect of ethyl group elevates 3° over 1°, but steric hindrance still keeps 2° at the top.

B. Basicity in Gas Phase or Non-Polar Solvents

R3N > R2NH > RNH2 > NH3 (Order: 3° > 2° > 1° > NH3)

Reason: Solvation is absent; the order is governed purely by the electron-donating inductive effect (+I) dispersing positive charge in the conjugate ammonium cation.

C. Aromatic vs Aliphatic Amines

Cyclohexylamine > Benzylamine > NH3 > Aniline > Diphenylamine > Triphenylamine

Reason: In aniline, the nitrogen lone pair participates in resonance with the π-cloud of the benzene ring (5 resonating structures), decreasing electron availability. In benzylamine, the −CH2− spacer prevents resonance, behaving as a typical aliphatic amine.

D. Substituted Anilines: Ortho Effect

Any ortho-substituted aniline (regardless of whether the group is electron-donating or electron-withdrawing, e.g., −CH3, −NO2, −OCH3, −Cl) is significantly weaker base than aniline.

Reason: Steric Inhibition of Protonation (SIP). Protonation produces the bulky −NH3+ group, which suffers intense steric crowding with the ortho substituent, destroying cation hydration and destabilizing the conjugate acid.

E. Boiling Point Comparison

Primary amine (R−NH2) > Secondary amine (R2NH) > Tertiary amine (R3N) (for isomeric amines)

Reason: 1° amines have 2 acidic N−H bonds enabling extensive intermolecular H-bonding; 2° amines have only 1 N−H bond; 3° amines have no N−H bond and cannot undergo self hydrogen-bonding (associate only by dipole-dipole interactions).

5. Common Traps & Exam Exceptions

Trap 1: Direct Nitration of Aniline Produces Significant Meta Product!
Direct treatment of aniline with conc. HNO3 + conc. H2SO4 gives 51% para, 47% meta, and 2% ortho. In strongly acidic medium, aniline is extensively protonated to form the anilinium ion (−NH3+), which exerts a powerful −I deactivating effect and directs the incoming nitronium electrophile to the meta position. To obtain p-nitroaniline cleanly, the −NH2 group must first be protected by acetylation (Ac2O/pyridine).
Trap 2: Aniline Does NOT Undergo Friedel-Crafts Reactions!
Aniline does not undergo Friedel-Crafts alkylation or acylation. The Lewis acid catalyst (anhydrous AlCl3) acts as a strong electron acceptor and coordinates directly with the highly basic lone pair of −NH2 to form an adduct: Ar−NH2: → AlCl3. This places a formal positive charge on nitrogen, deactivating the benzene ring toward electrophilic attack.
Trap 3: Bromine Water vs Bromine in Non-Polar Solvent on Aniline
Aniline with Br2/H2O produces a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromoaniline instantaneously without any catalyst because −NH2 massively activates the ring and water ionizes the intermediate rapidly. Monobromination requires protecting the −NH2 group as acetanilide, brominating with Br2/CH3COOH to yield p-bromoacetanilide, followed by acidic or alkaline hydrolysis.
Trap 4: Diazonium Salt with Potassium Iodide Needs NO Copper Catalyst!
While Sandmeyer chlorination/bromination requires CuCl/CuBr, iodination simply requires warming [ArN2+] with aqueous KI alone. The iodide ion (I−) is a strong enough reducing agent to initiate electron-transfer homolysis to the aryl radical directly without metal catalysis.
Trap 5: Cope Elimination vs Hofmann Elimination Regiochemistry
Pyrolysis of tertiary amine oxides (Cope Elimination) proceeds via a concerted 5-membered cyclic transition state with syn-stereochemistry. In contrast, quaternary ammonium hydroxide pyrolysis (Hofmann Elimination) proceeds via an anti-periplanar E2 transition state. Both yield the less substituted (Hofmann) alkene.

6. JEE Advanced Practice Problems

Q1 (Conceptual): Why is trimethylamine less basic than dimethylamine in water despite having three +I methyl groups?
Explanation: Basicity in aqueous solution depends on the stabilization of the conjugate cation [RnNH4-n]+. While trimethylamine has three +I groups increasing electron density, its conjugate cation (CH3)3NH+ has only one N−H bond capable of forming hydrogen bonds with water and suffers from steric crowding that shields the positive charge from water molecules. In contrast, (CH3)2NH2+ has two N−H bonds available for extensive hydration. The hydration energy loss in (CH3)3N outweighs the marginal +I gain, rendering (CH3)2NH the stronger base.
Q2 (Conceptual): What accounts for the non-resolvability of chiral ethylmethylamine at 25°C?
Explanation: Ethylmethylamine [CH3CH2−NH−CH3] possesses an sp3 hybridized nitrogen with three distinct groups and one lone pair, making it technically chiral. However, it undergoes rapid pyramidal inversion (umbrella inversion) through a planar sp2-hybridized transition state. Because the activation barrier is low (~25 kJ/mol), interconversion between enantiomers occurs millions of times per second at room temperature, producing a non-separable racemic mixture. (Note: Quaternary ammonium salts [N(R1)(R2)(R3)(R4)]+ or Tröger's base cannot invert and are resolvable).
Q3 (Conceptual): Explain why azo coupling with phenol requires pH 9–10, whereas coupling with aniline requires pH 4–5.
Explanation: The arenediazonium ion [ArN2+] is a weak electrophile and requires a strongly activated ring for attack.
• Phenol: At pH 9–10, phenol is converted to phenoxide ion (PhO−), whose +M activating effect is far superior to neutral PhOH. Above pH 10, the diazonium ion reacts with hydroxide to form non-electrophilic diazoate [Ar−N=N−O−].
• Aniline: At pH 4–5, aniline exists primarily as unprotonated, nucleophilic free base PhNH2. If pH drops below 3, aniline is converted to unreactive anilinium ion PhNH3+. If pH is basic (>7), coupling occurs on the nitrogen atom rather than the aromatic carbon, forming diazoamino compounds (triazenes).
Q4 (Conceptual): Why does Gabriel Phthalimide synthesis fail for the preparation of aromatic primary amines like aniline?
Explanation: The second step of Gabriel synthesis requires an SN2 displacement of the halide by the bulky phthalimide anion. Aryl halides (such as chlorobenzene) do not undergo SN2 reactions due to:
1. Partial double-bond character of the C−X bond arising from resonance with the benzene ring.
2. Electrostatic repulsion between the nucleophile and the electron-rich aromatic π-cloud.
3. Inability to achieve backside attack with inversion on an sp2 carbon.
Q5 (Product Prediction): Identify product P: (R)-2-phenylpropanamide + Br2 + KOH (Δ) → P.
Answer: (R)-1-phenylethanamine [Ph−CH(CH3)−NH2].
Explanation: This is the Hofmann bromamide degradation. The migrating group (the chiral α-phenylethyl group) migrates intramolecularly to the electron-deficient nitrogen in a concerted step. Hence, the migration occurs with 100% retention of stereochemical configuration.
Q6 (Product Prediction): Aniline + NaNO2 + HCl (0–5°C) followed by reaction with HBF4 gives solid A. Heating solid A with NaNO2 in presence of Cu powder gives product B. Identify A and B.
Answer: A = Benzenediazonium fluoroborate [PhN2+ BF4−], B = Nitrobenzene [PhNO2].
Explanation: Diazotization followed by addition of fluoroboric acid precipitates the stable, water-insoluble arenediazonium fluoroborate salt. Heating this isolated salt with aqueous sodium nitrite in the presence of catalytic copper powder replaces the diazonium group with a nitro group via a radical pathway.
Q7 (Product Prediction): What is the major alkene formed when N,N,N-trimethyl-2-pentanammonium hydroxide is heated?
Answer: 1-Pentene.
Explanation: Pyrolysis of quaternary ammonium hydroxides follows the Hofmann elimination rule. The sterically demanding leaving group [−N(CH3)3] creates high steric strain in the anti-periplanar conformation needed to remove the more substituted β-proton (C3). Thus, base abstracts the most acidic, least hindered β-proton from C1, yielding the terminal alkene (1-pentene) as the major product rather than the Zaitsev alkene (2-pentene).
Q8 (Multi-Step): Benzene → [HNO3/H2SO4] → A → [Cl2/FeCl3] → B → [Sn/HCl] → C → [NaNO2/HCl, 0–5°C] → D → [H3PO2/H2O] → E. Identify E.
Answer: Chlorobenzene.
Steps:
1. Benzene → Nitrobenzene (A).
2. Nitro group is meta-directing: Nitrobenzene + Cl2/FeCl3 → 1-chloro-3-nitrobenzene (B).
3. Selective reduction: B + Sn/HCl → 3-chloroaniline (C).
4. Diazotization: C + NaNO2/HCl → 3-chlorobenzenediazonium chloride (D).
5. Reductive deamination: D + H3PO2 removes the −N2+ group replacing it with −H, yielding chlorobenzene (E).
Q9 (Comparison / Multi-Factor): Arrange the following in decreasing order of pKb: (I) Aniline, (II) p-Nitroaniline, (III) p-Toluidine, (IV) p-Methoxyaniline.
Answer: II > I > III > IV.
Explanation: Remember that higher pKb means weaker basicity.
• Basicity order: p-Methoxyaniline (IV, strong +M) > p-Toluidine (III, +I and hyperconjugation) > Aniline (I) > p-Nitroaniline (II, powerful −M and −I deactivates lone pair).
• Inverting this to find pKb (least basic has highest pKb): II > I > III > IV.
Q10 (Multi-Step Synthesis): Cyclohexanecarboxamide is treated with: (i) Br2/KOH (Δ), (ii) excess CH3I, (iii) wet Ag2O and heat. Predict the final product.
Answer: Cyclohexene.
Steps:
1. Cyclohexanecarboxamide undergoes Hofmann degradation to lose the carbonyl carbon, producing cyclohexanamine.
2. Exhaustive methylation with excess CH3I converts it to N,N,N-trimethylcyclohexylammonium iodide.
3. Wet Ag2O exchanges iodide for hydroxide, producing the quaternary ammonium hydroxide. Subsequent heating triggers anti-periplanar E2 elimination to yield cyclohexene, trimethylamine, and water.
