Chemistry in Everyday Life

Class 12 | JEE Advanced

Chapter summary

Chemistry in Everyday Life connects molecular structure with medicines, cleansing agents, food additives and chemicals used in daily life. Drugs act by interacting with biological targets such as enzymes, receptors and ion channels, so functional groups and molecular shape strongly influence their action. Important drug classes include antacids, antihistamines, analgesics, antimicrobials, antiseptics and disinfectants. Soaps and detergents remove oily dirt through micelle formation, while water hardness affects soap efficiency. JEE-level questions often test definitions, drug classification, mechanisms of action, structures, pH effects, and comparisons between soaps and synthetic detergents.

JEE idea: Do not confuse an antacid with a drug that decreases acid secretion. Antacids primarily neutralise acid already present in the stomach.

Antihistamines

Histamine participates in allergic and inflammatory responses. Antihistamine drugs reduce the effects of histamine by interacting with histamine receptors. Many classical antihistamines act as H1-receptor antagonists or inverse agonists depending on the pharmacological classification used.

Soaps: structure and cleansing action

Soaps are generally sodium or potassium salts of higher fatty acids. A typical soap molecule contains a long non-polar hydrocarbon chain and an ionic carboxylate head:

R−COO− Na+    where R is a long hydrocarbon chain.
Part of soapNatureInteraction
Hydrocarbon chain, RHydrophobic / non-polarInteracts with grease and oily dirt.
Carboxylate head, −COO−Hydrophilic / ionicInteracts strongly with water.

In water, soap molecules can aggregate into micelles. Their hydrocarbon tails point toward the oily material while ionic heads remain in contact with water. Agitation helps detach and disperse oily dirt, which can then be washed away.

Soap preparation: saponification

Fats and oils are mainly triglycerides. Heating a triglyceride with a strong base such as NaOH or KOH produces glycerol and salts of fatty acids:

Triglyceride + 3NaOH → Glycerol + 3RCOONa
TermMeaning
SaponificationAlkaline hydrolysis of fats/oils producing glycerol and fatty-acid salts.
Hard soapGenerally sodium salts of fatty acids.
Soft soapGenerally potassium salts of fatty acids.

Soaps in hard water

Hard water contains appreciable Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions. These ions react with soap anions to form insoluble calcium and magnesium salts, commonly called soap scum.

2RCOO− + Ca2+ → (RCOO)2Ca ↓

This removes soap from solution and reduces lathering and cleansing efficiency.

Micelles and cleansing mechanism

StageWhat happens
1. WettingWater contacts the surface and detergent molecules distribute at interfaces.
2. AdsorptionHydrophobic portions associate with oily dirt while hydrophilic groups remain oriented toward water.
3. Micelle formationAbove the critical micelle concentration, surfactant molecules aggregate into micelles.
4. DispersionAgitation breaks up and disperses oily material within surfactant aggregates.
5. RinsingWater carries the dispersed material away.
The term critical micelle concentration (CMC) refers to the concentration above which micelles form appreciably. The exact CMC depends on the surfactant and conditions.

Common traps and exceptions

Trap 1: An antibiotic is not synonymous with an antiseptic. Antibiotics are drugs used against susceptible bacteria, whereas antiseptics are intended for suitable applications on living tissues.
Trap 2: Soap cleaning becomes inefficient in hard water because Ca2+ and Mg2+ form insoluble fatty-acid salts.
Trap 3: Fragrance, cleansing action and disinfecting action are different concepts; a pleasant-smelling substance is not automatically a disinfectant.
Trap 4: Detergents are not necessarily non-biodegradable. Structure determines biodegradability.
Trap 5: Antacids neutralise existing stomach acid; they should not be confused with all drugs that reduce acid secretion.
Trap 6: An enzyme inhibitor can act by several mechanisms. Do not label every inhibitor as competitive unless it actually competes for the relevant active site.

Practice questions

1. Which statements are correct? (Multiple correct) (A) Soaps are generally salts of higher fatty acids. (B) Soap solutions are generally effective in hard water. (C) Synthetic detergents can be effective in hard water. (D) Soap scum can contain calcium carboxylates.

Answer: A, C and D.

Solution: Soaps are fatty-acid salts. Ca2+ and Mg2+ react with soap anions to form insoluble salts, so soaps are inefficient in hard water. Many synthetic detergents do not suffer from this problem to the same extent.

2. What is the main chemical reason that soap becomes less effective in hard water?

Answer: Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions form insoluble salts with soap anions.

Solution: For example, 2RCOO− + Ca2+ → (RCOO)2Ca↓. The precipitate removes surfactant from the solution and reduces micelle formation/cleansing efficiency.

3. Which drug is correctly matched with its class? (A) Sodium bicarbonate — antacid (B) Aspirin — antibiotic (C) Penicillin — antacid (D) Saccharin — antibiotic

Answer: A.

Solution: Sodium bicarbonate neutralises gastric HCl and acts as an antacid. Aspirin is an analgesic/antipyretic with antiplatelet action, penicillin is an antibiotic, and saccharin is an artificial sweetener.

4. Which statements about drug-receptor/enzyme interactions are correct? (Multiple correct) (A) Receptors can act as biological targets. (B) Competitive inhibitors can compete with a substrate for an active site. (C) Every drug acts only by forming a covalent bond with its target. (D) Molecular shape can influence drug-target recognition.

Answer: A, B and D.

Solution: Receptors are common drug targets, and competitive inhibitors compete at an enzyme active site. Drug-target interactions commonly involve non-covalent forces; covalent bonding occurs for some drugs but is not universal. Complementarity of molecular shape and functional groups is important.

5. Arrange the following according to the number of hydrogen-bond donors directly present in the molecule: aspirin, paracetamol, acetic acid. Give the increasing order.

Answer: Aspirin < paracetamol = acetic acid.

Solution: Aspirin has no O−H or N−H donor because its phenolic OH is acetylated; paracetamol has one phenolic O−H and acetic acid has one carboxylic O−H.

6. Compare the behaviour of sodium stearate soap and a typical anionic sulfonate detergent in hard water. Which is expected to retain better cleansing action, and why?

Answer: The sulfonate detergent generally retains better cleansing action because its Ca2+/Mg2+ salts are much more soluble than the insoluble calcium/magnesium soaps formed from fatty-acid carboxylates.

Solution: Soap anions form precipitates such as (RCOO)2Ca, removing surfactant from solution. Sulfonate detergents generally avoid this severe precipitation problem.

7. A student adds excess NaOH to a triglyceride and heats the mixture. Identify the principal organic products and the reaction type.

Answer: Glycerol and sodium salts of fatty acids; the reaction is saponification, i.e. alkaline hydrolysis of the ester groups.

Solution: Each triglyceride contains three ester linkages. Hydrolysis with NaOH converts the three acyl portions into RCOO−Na+ and releases glycerol.

8. A cleaning formulation contains a long hydrocarbon chain attached to an ionic sulfonate group. Predict the orientation of these molecules around an oil droplet in water and explain the result.

Answer: The hydrocarbon chains orient toward the oil while the sulfonate groups remain in contact with water.

Solution: The hydrocarbon portion is hydrophobic and the sulfonate group is hydrophilic/ionic. Aggregation therefore disperses oily material in water and facilitates its removal during rinsing.

9. A patient takes an antacid containing Mg(OH)2. Write the balanced neutralisation equation with gastric HCl and determine the mole ratio of HCl to Mg(OH)2.

Answer: Mg(OH)2 + 2HCl → MgCl2 + 2H2O; mole ratio HCl : Mg(OH)2 = 2 : 1.

Solution: Mg(OH)2 contains two hydroxide groups, each neutralising one mole of HCl.

10. A detergent works well in hard water, while a soap gives precipitate. The water contains Ca2+. A surfactant has the general formula RSO3−. Explain why the detergent can remain effective and identify its head-group type.

Answer: RSO3− is an anionic sulfonate surfactant. Its calcium salt is generally sufficiently soluble that the severe precipitation problem characteristic of fatty-acid soaps is avoided.

Solution: Soap has RCOO− heads that form insoluble calcium/magnesium carboxylates. Sulfonate detergents are designed so that hardness ions do not remove the surfactant from solution to the same extent.
