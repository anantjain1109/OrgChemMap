Polymers

Class 12 | JEE Advanced

Core ideas

Polymers are high-molar-mass macromolecules formed by joining many small molecules called monomers. JEE-level polymer chemistry focuses on classification, mechanisms of polymer formation, monomer identification, repeat units, copolymers, important synthetic and natural polymers, and the structural basis of their properties.

Addition (chain-growth) polymerisation generally uses unsaturated monomers and does not eliminate a small molecule, whereas condensation (step-growth) polymerisation joins bifunctional or polyfunctional monomers with elimination of small molecules such as H2O or HCl. Copolymers contain more than one kind of monomer.

Major polymerisation mechanisms

MechanismSteps in orderRate-determining stepWhat favours it
Free-radical chain polymerisation1. Initiator decomposes to radicals.
2. Radical adds to the C=C bond of a monomer, generating a new chain radical.
3. Repeated propagation adds monomers.
4. Termination occurs by combination or disproportionation.		There is no universal single RDS for all radical polymerisations; initiation and propagation rates depend on the system.Radical initiators such as peroxides/AIBN, suitable temperature, and monomers capable of stabilising the growing radical.
Cationic chain polymerisation1. Electrophilic initiation generates a carbocationic chain end.
2. Monomer attacks the carbocation.
3. The new carbocation propagates by further monomer addition.
4. Chain termination occurs by nucleophilic capture, proton loss, or another suitable pathway.		Propagation/termination depend strongly on the particular monomer and catalyst; no single universal RDS should be assigned.Electron-rich alkenes and strong Lewis/Brønsted acids; conditions that stabilise the carbocationic chain end.
Anionic chain polymerisation1. Strong nucleophile/base generates a carbanionic chain end.
2. Carbanion attacks the monomer.
3. The new carbanion continues propagation.
4. Chain termination occurs by protonation or another electrophilic quench.		No universal RDS; propagation and initiation depend on the monomer/initiator pair.Monomers whose substituents stabilise a carbanion and strong nucleophilic/basic initiators under suitable conditions.
Coordination polymerisation1. Monomer coordinates to a metal centre.
2. Monomer inserts into the metal-carbon bond of the growing chain.
3. Repeated coordination/insertion gives the polymer chain.
4. Chain transfer or termination stops growth.		Mechanism and kinetic bottleneck depend on catalyst system; do not assume a universal RDS.Ziegler-Natta-type catalysts or related coordination catalysts; especially useful for controlling stereochemistry of substituted alkenes.
Condensation (step-growth) polymerisation1. Functional groups of monomers react.
2. A dimer forms.
3. Oligomers react with monomers/other oligomers.
4. Repeated functional-group reactions produce high polymer; small molecules may be eliminated.		No universal RDS; the rate depends on functional-group reactivity, catalyst, temperature, and removal of condensate.Bifunctional/polyfunctional monomers, high conversion, appropriate catalyst/temperature, and removal of small molecules when applicable.

Comparisons and orders

ComparisonOrder / distinctionReason
Chain-growth vs step-growthChain-growth: active chain end adds monomer rapidly; step-growth: any two suitable functional species can reactChain-growth proceeds through active centres, whereas step-growth proceeds through functional-group reactions between monomers/oligomers.
Addition vs condensationAddition: generally no small-molecule elimination; condensation: often eliminates H2O, HCl, etc.Determined by the functionality and reaction pathway of the monomers.
Thermoplastic vs thermosettingThermoplastic: generally linear/branched and remouldable; thermosetting: extensively cross-linked and not remouldable on heatingCross-links prevent the polymer chains from flowing freely after curing.
Elastomer vs fibreElastomers: flexible chains with limited cross-linking; fibres: strong intermolecular interactions and more ordered chainsChain mobility, intermolecular forces, crystallinity and cross-linking determine mechanical behaviour.
Natural vs synthetic rubberNatural rubber: cis-polyisoprene; synthetic rubbers may use different monomers/compositionsMonomer structure and microstructure control elasticity and resistance properties.
Natural rubber vs gutta-perchaNatural rubber: predominantly cis-1,4-polyisoprene; gutta-percha: predominantly trans-1,4-polyisopreneCis/trans geometry changes chain packing and therefore physical properties.
Polymer stability during vulcanisationUncross-linked rubber < suitably cross-linked vulcanised rubber for dimensional stabilitySulfur cross-links restrict chain slippage and improve strength and resilience.
Important: Polymer molecular mass is usually an average quantity because a polymer sample contains chains of different lengths. Do not treat n as indicating that every chain has exactly the same molecular mass.
Tacticity trap: Isotactic means substituents are arranged regularly on the same side in the idealised chain representation; syndiotactic means alternating arrangement; atactic means irregular arrangement.
Condensation trap: Not every polymer made from a functional-group-containing monomer is automatically a condensation polymer. Nylon-6 is formed by ring-opening polymerisation of ε-caprolactam, whereas Nylon-6,6 is a condensation polyamide.
Copolymer trap: Buna-S and Buna-N are copolymers because two different monomers contribute to the polymer chain. A polymer formed from only one monomer is a homopolymer.
Vulcanisation trap: Sulfur does not simply increase the molecular mass by ordinary chain extension. It introduces cross-links between rubber chains, changing elasticity, strength and dimensional stability.
Natural rubber trap: Natural rubber is predominantly cis-1,4-polyisoprene. The trans form, gutta-percha, has substantially different packing and physical behaviour.
Biodegradable polymer: PHBV is a commonly cited biodegradable copolymer. Its ester linkages are susceptible to biological/chemical degradation, unlike many conventional carbon-backbone addition polymers.

Practice questions

1. Conceptual: Why can a thermosetting polymer not normally be remoulded by heating, whereas a thermoplastic can?Thermosetting polymers contain extensive covalent cross-linking between chains, producing a three-dimensional network. Heating does not simply allow the chains to slide past one another; sufficiently strong heating eventually causes degradation. Thermoplastics have comparatively weak intermolecular interactions between separate chains and can soften and flow on heating.
2. Conceptual: Distinguish addition polymerisation from condensation polymerisation using the nature of the repeating process rather than only the monomers.In addition polymerisation, unsaturated monomers add to a growing chain without eliminating a small molecule in each propagation event. In condensation polymerisation, polymer growth occurs through reactions of functional groups and commonly involves elimination of small molecules such as H2O or HCl.
3. Conceptual: Why does the cis configuration of natural rubber favour elastomeric behaviour compared with the trans form of polyisoprene?Cis-1,4-polyisoprene has a kinked chain geometry that reduces efficient close packing and allows substantial chain flexibility. Trans-1,4-polyisoprene can pack more regularly and is therefore much less elastomeric.
4. Conceptual: A polymer sample has chains with different degrees of polymerisation. Why is it inappropriate to assign one exact molecular mass to the whole sample?Different chains have different lengths and therefore different molecular masses. Polymer samples are consequently described using average molar masses, such as number-average and weight-average molar mass, rather than a single exact molecular mass for every molecule.
5. Product prediction: Identify the polymer and repeating unit obtained from CH2=CHCl by addition polymerisation.The product is poly(vinyl chloride), PVC. Its repeating unit is –[CH2–CHCl]n–. The C=C bond of vinyl chloride is consumed during chain growth.
6. Product prediction: Hexamethylenediamine is polymerised with adipic acid. What type of linkage appears in the polymer, and what small molecule is eliminated?Nylon-6,6 is formed. The characteristic linkage is an amide linkage, –CO–NH–. Water is eliminated during formation of each amide bond in the idealised condensation process.
7. Product prediction: What polymer is expected from tetrafluoroethene, CF2=CF2, by addition polymerisation?PTFE (Teflon), with repeating unit –[CF2–CF2]n–. No small molecule is eliminated because this is addition polymerisation.
8. Multi-step: A material is prepared from phenol and formaldehyde and then extensively cross-linked. Identify the polymer class and explain why the final material behaves differently from a thermoplastic.The material is a phenol-formaldehyde resin such as Bakelite. Extensive cross-linking creates a three-dimensional thermosetting network. The covalent network strongly restricts chain motion, so the material does not simply soften and remould on heating like a thermoplastic.
9. Comparison: Arrange the following according to the number of different monomers used: polyethylene, Buna-S, Buna-N. Which are copolymers?Polyethylene uses one monomer, ethene, so it is a homopolymer. Buna-S uses butadiene and styrene, while Buna-N uses butadiene and acrylonitrile; both Buna-S and Buna-N are copolymers because two different monomers are incorporated.
10. Multi-step/comparison: A sample contains natural rubber and is then treated with sulfur to introduce cross-links. What structural change occurs, and how does it affect the material?Sulfur forms cross-links between polymer chains. These cross-links reduce permanent chain slippage and improve tensile strength, elasticity over a useful temperature range, abrasion resistance and dimensional stability. Excessive cross-linking, however, can make the material harder and less elastomeric.
