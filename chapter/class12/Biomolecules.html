Biomolecules

Class 12 | JEE Advanced

Chapter summary

Biomolecules are naturally occurring organic compounds that build cells and carry out biological functions. Carbohydrates include monosaccharides, oligosaccharides and polysaccharides, with stereochemistry and reducing behaviour being especially important. Amino acids form peptides and proteins through peptide bonds, while proteins show hierarchical structures and can undergo denaturation. Nucleic acids contain nucleotides joined by phosphodiester bonds and store or transmit genetic information. Vitamins and enzymes are important functional biomolecules, with enzyme activity depending strongly on structure and conditions.

Protein structure and properties

PrimarySpecific sequence of amino-acid residues.Peptide bonds determine the covalent chain.

Common traps and exceptions

Trap 1: Fructose is a ketose but is still a reducing sugar because it can form aldose species under the conditions of common reducing-sugar tests.
Trap 2: Sucrose is non-reducing because its glycosidic linkage involves the anomeric carbon of glucose and the anomeric carbon of fructose.
Trap 3: Anomers are not simply "all stereoisomers"; they differ specifically at the anomeric carbon.
Trap 4: A nucleoside has no phosphate group. Adding phosphate gives a nucleotide.
Trap 5: Denaturation does not normally mean hydrolysis of every peptide bond; the primary covalent sequence is generally retained.
Trap 6: Enzymes change reaction rates and activation barriers, but they do not change the thermodynamic equilibrium constant of a reaction.

Practice questions

1. Which statements about carbohydrates are correct? (Multiple correct)
(A) Glucose is an aldose. (B) Fructose is a ketose. (C) Sucrose is reducing. (D) Cellulose contains β(1→4) glycosidic linkages.

Answer: A, B and D.

Solution: Glucose contains an aldehyde group in its open-chain form, fructose contains a ketone group, and cellulose is a polymer of glucose with β(1→4) linkages. Sucrose is non-reducing because both anomeric carbons participate in its glycosidic bond.

2. What is mutarotation? Why can glucose show it?

Answer: Mutarotation is the change in optical rotation caused by interconversion of α and β anomers through the open-chain form. Glucose can show it because its cyclic hemiacetal can open and close, allowing the configuration at the anomeric carbon to change.

3. Which statement correctly distinguishes a nucleoside from a nucleotide? (A) Nucleoside = sugar + base; nucleotide = sugar + base + phosphate. (B) Nucleoside contains a peptide bond. (C) Nucleotide contains no sugar. (D) Nucleoside is always a polysaccharide.

Answer: A.

Solution: A nitrogenous base bonded to a pentose gives a nucleoside. Addition of one or more phosphate groups gives a nucleotide.

4. Which of the following are reducing sugars? (Multiple correct) Glucose, fructose, sucrose, maltose.

Answer: Glucose, fructose and maltose.

Solution: Glucose has an accessible open-chain aldehyde equilibrium, fructose can form aldoses under basic test conditions, and maltose has one free anomeric carbon. Sucrose has both anomeric carbons involved in the glycosidic linkage.

5. Arrange the following in increasing number of hydrogen bonds in their standard DNA base pair: A···T and G···C.

Answer: A···T < G···C.

Solution: A···T contains two hydrogen bonds, whereas G···C contains three.

6. A carbohydrate gives a positive reducing-sugar test but contains no free aldehyde group in its predominant cyclic structure. Explain.

Answer: A cyclic carbohydrate can exist in equilibrium with an open-chain form. For glucose, the cyclic hemiacetal can open to generate the aldehyde form, allowing reduction of suitable reagents. Therefore, the predominant cyclic structure need not itself contain a free aldehyde group.

7. A peptide contains the sequence Gly–Ala–Val. How many peptide bonds connect these three amino-acid residues, and what is the direction of the sequence conventionally written?

Answer: Two peptide bonds; the sequence is conventionally written from the N-terminus to the C-terminus.

Solution: Three amino-acid residues require two links between adjacent residues. The conventional direction is N-terminal residue → C-terminal residue.

8. A solution contains a mixture of α-D-glucose and β-D-glucose. It is allowed to stand in water. What happens to the composition and optical rotation?

Answer: The two anomers interconvert through the open-chain form until equilibrium is reached, so the optical rotation changes toward its equilibrium value.

Solution: Opening of the cyclic hemiacetal temporarily gives the open-chain form, which can close to either anomer. This interconversion is mutarotation.

9. A DNA segment has the sequence 5′-AGCT-3′. Write its complementary strand in antiparallel orientation and determine the total number of hydrogen bonds in the four base pairs.

Answer: Complementary strand: 3′-TCGA-5′. Total hydrogen bonds = 10.

Solution: A pairs with T and G pairs with C. The segment contains two A···T pairs (2 bonds each) and two G···C pairs (3 bonds each): 2(2) + 2(3) = 10.

10. A protein loses its biological activity after heating, but analysis shows that its peptide backbone has not been hydrolysed. What process is most consistent with this observation, and which structural levels are primarily affected?

Answer: Denaturation; mainly secondary, tertiary and/or quaternary structure can be disrupted while the primary sequence may remain intact.

Solution: Protein function depends on its three-dimensional organisation. Heating can disrupt hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions and other non-covalent interactions, causing loss of the native conformation without necessarily cleaving peptide bonds.
